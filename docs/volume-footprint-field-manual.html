The altimeter for order flow.

A complete operating manual for VFP‑Intro: how the instrument builds every number it shows you, how to fly it, where it lies to you, and exactly what to change in the source to make it lie less.

Chapter 01 — Part I

What this instrument actually is

A real footprint chart is a recording. This one is, for most people who will run it, a reconstruction — and knowing which one you are looking at is the difference between an edge and a hallucination.

Strip away the tables and the bells and VFP‑Intro does one thing: it takes a single number per bar — volume — splits it into a buy half and a sell half, then decides at which prices inside the bar each half happened. Everything else in the script is presentation or arithmetic on top of that one decision.

The honesty of the whole instrument therefore collapses to one question: where did the split come from? There are three answers, and they are not equally true. Chapter 02 is entirely about that hierarchy, and it is the most important chapter in the book.

The signal chain

Every figure you will ever read — a cell, a POC, an OVL coefficient, an alert — is downstream of the same four stages. Learn this chain and you can always answer “why is this number doing that?”

What it is not

  • Not a tape reader. Even in Footprint mode it never shows you individual trades, aggressor size, or iceberg refills.
  • Not a historical study. The table and profile are rebuilt from scratch on the last bar of the chart. Scroll back and you see nothing. There is no bar-by-bar history to eyeball or backtest.
  • Not a fixed-range profile. The frame is anchored to the current price and slides with it, tick by tick. A market-profile practitioner will find this deeply strange at first; Chapter 04 explains why it is the right choice for a live instrument and the wrong choice for structural analysis.
  • Not a signal generator. There is exactly one hardcoded condition in the entire script — the OVL balance threshold at 0.75. Everything else is levels and numbers you must interpret.
Read this first

If you take one thing from Part I: the profile is always drawn from the geometric bell model, even when the engine is set to Footprint. The engine changes the totals that feed each bell; it never changes the shape. Chapter 24, trap 01 covers this in full — it is the single largest gap between what the instrument appears to say and what it knows.

Chapter 02 — Part I

The three engines, ranked by truth

Three sources, three different epistemic statuses. One is measurement, one is inference, one is a geometric guess dressed in convincing mathematics.

Footprint — measurement

request.footprint() asks TradingView's server for the same per-tick data behind its native footprint charts. Each bar returns a volume_row array: for every price row, the real buy volume, the real sell volume, and the platform's own imbalance verdict. This is the genuine article. It requires a Premium or Ultimate plan; on a lower plan the script errors out rather than substituting anything.

The Ticks Per Row input only exists for this engine. At 1 every minimum price increment gets its own row and the footprint lattice lines up exactly with the altimeter lattice — which is why the code reads rows by up_price(), so a trade at the live price lands on the live price's own row instead of the row below it.

Intrabar — inference

tvta.requestUpAndDownVolume() drops to a lower timeframe and classifies each of those candles as up or down, summing volume accordingly. This is real volume, classified by a real rule — but the rule is “did the 1-minute candle close higher than it opened”, not “did this trade hit the bid or the ask.” A 1‑minute candle that opens at 100, spikes to 105, and closes at 99.9 contributes all its volume to the sell side.

Its hard limit is history. Your plan's intrabar allowance decides how many chart bars can be filled; older bars return na and the script refuses to substitute. The banner's DATA n BARS figure is your honest coverage readout.

Geometric — model

The default, and the only engine that works everywhere on all history. It computes

// buy share of the bar's volume
geoShare = (close - low) / (high - low)   // 0.5 when range == 0
engBuy   = volume * geoShare
engSell  = volume * (1 - geoShare)

That is the classical close-location-value split. It is defensible as a heuristic and indefensible as data. Its failure modes are specific and worth memorising:

  • A bar closing exactly on its low reports zero buy volume. No market has ever traded with zero buyers.
  • A wide bar that ran up, reversed, and closed mid-range reports a clean 50/50 — identical to a quiet doji that never moved.
  • Gaps and auction opens are invisible: the split only sees H, L and C.

The FX trap

Most spot FX symbols publish tick count or nothing at all instead of traded volume. The script draws a red panel when volume is na, but a symbol reporting tick count reports a plausible-looking number that has no size behind it. Every figure in the instrument then measures activity, not size. Use futures (6E, 6B, M6E) instead of spot pairs.

Chapter 03 — Part I

The bell model: how one bar becomes a column of numbers

Two truncated normal distributions per bar — one for buying, one for selling — placed by a rule you can state in a sentence, and integrated exactly rather than sampled.

Given a bar with low L, high H, close C, and its engine split into bv buy and sv sell:

μbuy  = L + (C − L)/2      // midpoint of the journey up to the close
μsell = C + (H − C)/2      // midpoint of the journey above the close
σ     = (H − L) / Concentration
truncated to [L, H], renormalised by its own truncation mass

The intuition the author has encoded: buying is what carried price from the low up to where it settled; selling is what capped it above the settle. On an up bar the buy bell is fat and low, the sell bell is squeezed into the upper wick. On a down bar the geometry inverts. It is a story about the bar, told in two curves.

Why “exact” matters here

Most footprint approximations bucket a bar's volume with a loop and then rescale so the buckets add up. This one doesn't. Each row's number is the closed-form truncated-normal mass of its own half-tick band, computed by cumulative distribution function differences with the boundary CDF carried forward between rows. Nothing is ever normalised after the fact, which is what allows the RES self-check in Chapter 08 to be meaningful at all: if the arithmetic were rescaled, the residual would be zero by construction and would tell you nothing.

Precision footnote

The table and the profile use different normal CDFs. The table calls f_normCdf (Abramowitz–Stegun 26.2.17, error < 7.5×10−8); the profile calls f_profNormCdf (Cody rational Chebyshev, error < 2×10−16). If you ever compare a Sum-column figure against a profile band mass to the last digit, that eight-orders-of-magnitude gap is the reason they differ. Chapter 27 has the two-line fix.

Flat bars are atoms, not bells

A bar with high == low has no range to spread across, so it becomes a point mass on a single lattice row. The profile keeps these as separate “atoms” outside the curves entirely and adds their matched minimum into the OVL term. On illiquid symbols and thin overnight sessions this is a large share of your bars — and it is the reason the OVL can read high on a market that simply wasn't trading.

Chapter 04 — Part I

The altimeter frame

The instrument does not show you a fixed slice of price. It shows you a fixed number of ticks around wherever price currently is — and the ladder slides behind the needle.

The frame is defined by two things: the axis row, which is the lattice index of the current close, and Ticks Above/Below, which sets how many one-tick rows sit on each side. Total height is always 2 × viewTicks + 1 rows. Nothing inside the frame is ever merged or aggregated — one tick, one row, always.

The consequence nobody warns you about

Because the frame slides, the dashboard POC is not a market structure level. It is a level relative to where price is standing right now. Price drifting up ten ticks can move the dashboard POC even though not one new trade occurred at the old POC. Traders coming from fixed-range volume profile read the W‑POC as “the level” and get burned by this within a week.

Two mental corrections make it usable:

  1. Read the W‑POC as “where the business is, near me” — a local centre of gravity within ±viewTicks, not a structural level. Its usefulness is short-horizon and it should be re-read continuously.
  2. Read the C‑POC as the structural level. The chart POC comes from the profile, whose vertical span is the period's own high-to-low range and does not slide with price. That is the level you mark on your chart.
Off-frame volume

The split functions return bOff and sOff — every unit of volume that fell outside the frame. The arithmetic uses them (rows + off-frame always re-add to the bar's real volume, which is what keeps the model honest), but nothing on screen ever shows them. On a wide-range bar with a small frame, the majority of that bar's volume can be invisible while its Total row still shows the full figure. Chapter 27 adds a two-line overflow indicator; until then, watch for columns whose Total dwarfs the sum of their visible cells.

Chapter 05 — Part I

Zone memory: why your column headers skip numbers

The window does not show you the last five bars. It shows you the last five bars that actually did business at the price you are standing on — and that is a genuinely different, genuinely better question.

The search walks backward from the current bar. For each candidate it asks three things: does the bar have data the active engine could fill, does its price range overlap the frame, and does it paint at least one visible cell? Bars that fail are skipped at almost no cost; bars that pass become a column. The walk stops when the window is full, at the first bar of the dataset, or at the 5000-bar buffer ceiling — whichever comes first.

The stale-column trap

Nothing stops the search from filling a column with a bar from three thousand bars ago. On a trending market that returns to an old level, your five “current” columns may include volume from last month, presented with the same visual weight as the bar that is printing right now. Always read the column headers. A jump from −1 to −847 is the instrument telling you it ran out of recent participation — which is itself a signal (you are at a price nobody has traded lately) but not the signal the layout implies. Chapter 27 adds a Max Column Age input in six lines.

The table allocates 4 + 2×Window columns and viewTicks×2 + 5 rows. At the maxima (60 bars, 50 ticks) that is 124×105 — over 13,000 cells, most of them empty, and unreadable on any screen. Practical ceiling for a desktop monitor at text size 12 is roughly 8 bars × 12 ticks.

Chapter 08 — Part I

The dual-bell profile, OVL and RES

To the right of the last bar, two mixture densities grown from one baseline — and two diagnostic numbers that most users ignore and shouldn’t.

Over Profile Period bars, each bar contributes its two bells as components of a mixture. The profile's vertical span is the period's own highest high to lowest low. The curve is sampled at Resolution points and drawn as a closed polyline; the widest price level reaches exactly Width bars into the free space. Both sides share one horizontal scale, so if the blue bell is visibly fatter than the red one, more buy volume genuinely occurred.

OVL — the overlap coefficient

OVL is the integral of the pointwise minimum of the two volume-normalised densities, plus the matched atomic masses of flat bars. It runs 0 to 1 and answers exactly one question: did both sides do their business at the same prices?

The label refuses to name a side unless |tilt| clears Balance Tilt % (default 5). That restraint matters: at OVL 0.4 with a tilt of 0.8%, the two sides are separated but neither is dominant — something is happening, but calling it “buy” would be a coin flip dressed as a read. The label says OFF BALANCE and stops.

RES — the residual, in parts per million

The profile integrates its own curves numerically (composite Simpson, cut at every bar boundary and every density crossing, with the crossings found by bisection to machine tolerance). It then checks: does the area under each curve, plus the flat-bar atoms, add back up to the volume that side actually traded? The gap is RES, reported in parts per million.

ReadingMeaningAction
< 0.01 PPMNormal. The drawing is a faithful picture of the volume.None
0.01 – 1 PPMStill inside default tolerance. Usually a symbol with awkward tick rounding.None
> tolerance — DRIFTIntegration has left its bounds. The shape may not reflect the volume.Do not trade the profile shape. Lower Resolution, shorten Period, or raise Concentration until it clears

RES is a health reading of the tool, not a signal about the market. It is also the fastest way to catch a bad configuration: an extremely low Concentration on a market with one enormous outlier bar can push sigma so wide that the truncation mass underflows, and RES will tell you long before the shape looks obviously wrong.

Chapter 09 — Part I

POC, Value Area, Imbalance — in two domains

Each metric exists twice, computed by different rules over different data. When they disagree, the disagreement is information.

MetricDashboard domainChart domain
POCFrame row with the greatest summed buy+sell across the windowSampled price where the combined curve height peaks, over the profile period
Value AreaExpansion from the window POC across frame rows until VA% of in-frame volume is capturedExpansion from the sampled POC across curve samples until VA% of curve area is captured
ImbalanceDiagonal rule on window row sums; first hit above and below the POCDiagonal rule on curve samples; first hit above and below the POC
Span±viewTicks around the live price — slidesPeriod’s high to low — fixed
Bars coveredWindow bars that traded in frameLast Profile Period bars, contiguous

The diagonal imbalance rule

Imbalance is never measured horizontally. A level is buy-imbalanced when its buy volume exceeds the sell volume of the level one tick below it, by the threshold percentage. Symmetrically, a level is sell-imbalanced when its sell volume exceeds the buy volume of the level one tick above. The default 300% demands three times the opposing volume.

Value-area expansion, step by step

Both domains run the classical algorithm: start at the POC, repeatedly look at the level immediately above and the level immediately below, take whichever holds more volume, add it to the running total, and repeat until you have captured VA%. The implementation is boundary-guarded — once a side runs out of levels it is never stepped again, so the loop always terminates.

The dashboard Value Area is truncated

The dashboard VA can only expand across rows that exist in the frame. If genuine value extends past ±viewTicks, the expansion hits the frame edge, stops, and reports a VAH or VAL that is really just “the edge of what I can see.” A VAH sitting exactly on the top frame row is almost always this artefact. Widen Ticks Above/Below until the VA edges pull in from the boundary, or read the chart VA instead — it has no such limit.

Chapter 10 — Part II

Ninety-second setup

Six decisions, in the order that matters. Everything else is default until you have a reason.

  1. Engine. Premium or Ultimate → Footprint, Ticks Per Row = 1. Otherwise, if your symbol is liquid and you only care about recent bars → Intrabar at a timeframe roughly 1/20 of your chart. Otherwise Geometric, and read Chapter 24 before you trust a single number.
  2. Confirm you have real volume. No red panel, and the banner’s VOL figure should look like size, not tick count. On Intrabar, check DATA n BARS covers at least your window.
  3. Ticks Above/Below. Set it so the frame spans roughly one to two average bar ranges. Chapter 12 has the arithmetic.
  4. Window Bars. Start at 5. Raise it only if your metrics column looks unstable tick to tick.
  5. Profile Period. Set it to a session, or a meaningful swing — not an arbitrary number. On a 5-minute chart, 78 bars is a US equity session; 23 (the default) is roughly two hours.
  6. Mobile Mode. Leave it on. Turn it off when investigating, not when trading.
Verify before you trust

After setup, glance at RES under the profile floor line. If it reads EXACT, the arithmetic behind everything you are about to look at is sound. If it reads DRIFT, fix that before reading anything else — see Chapter 25.

Chapter 12 — Part II

Sizing the frame and the window

Two inputs, one trade-off each, and a piece of arithmetic that removes the guesswork.

Ticks Above/Below — resolution versus coverage

The frame's total height in price is (2 × viewTicks + 1) × tickSize. Compare that against your market's typical bar range:

viewTicks ≈ (ATR(14) / tickSize) / 2      // frame ≈ one average bar
viewTicks ≈ (ATR(14) / tickSize)          // frame ≈ two average bars — preferred

Worked example, ES on a 5-minute chart: ATR ≈ 3.0 points, tick = 0.25 → 12 ticks per average bar → set viewTicks to 12–24. At 5 (the shipped default) the frame covers less than half an average bar, so most of every bar's volume falls off-frame and the Sum column stops resembling a profile.

Frame too narrowFrame about rightFrame too wide
Most cells filled, edges saturated; VA edges pinned to the frame boundary; huge gap between column Totals and visible cells Cells taper toward the edges; VA edges sit inside the frame; Totals roughly match visible cells Mostly empty rows; POC unstable because tiny volumes compete; table unreadable

Window Bars — stability versus recency

More columns means more volume in every window-scale figure, so the POC and Value Area stop flickering — at the cost of mixing older participation into a “now” reading. Because the window skips bars, calendar recency and column recency are different things.

  • 2–4: reactive, jumpy. Only for very fast scalping where you re-read every bar.
  • 5–8: the useful band for nearly everyone.
  • 10–15: stable levels for slower work, and the right choice on illiquid symbols where each bar contributes little.
  • 20+: you almost certainly want a longer Profile Period instead — the profile is contiguous and does not skip bars.
The relationship people miss

Window Bars and Profile Period are not two settings for the same thing. The window is discontiguous and price-anchored; the profile is contiguous and time-anchored. Use the window to ask “who has been trading here?” and the profile to ask “what has this period built?”

Chapter 13 — Part II

Tuning Volume Concentration

One number decides whether the model believes volume clusters at a bar’s centre or smears across its whole range. It has no correct value — it has a value that matches your market.

SettingSuitsSymptom that you set it wrong
1.0 – 2.0Trending, wide-range, gappy bars; markets that genuinely traverse their rangeValue Area spans nearly the whole frame; no level ever stands out
2.5 – 4.0Almost everything. The default 3.0 is a good prior—
5.0 – 10.0Balancing, rotational markets; small-range bars pinned around a meanPOC snaps violently between rows; imbalances fire everywhere; RES may drift

A practical calibration routine. With the profile visible, sweep Concentration from 1 to 6 and watch the bells. The right value is the lowest one at which a clear, single, stable peak emerges — below it the shape is a slab, above it you are manufacturing a peak that the bar ranges do not support.

A fixed reading order. Regime first, structure second, pressure third, entry last — never the other way round.

Scan discipline

The temptation is to read row 7 first because it changes every tick and feels like information. It is the least reliable number on the panel — in Geometric mode it is a function of where the forming bar happens to be sitting, and it will invert several times before the bar closes. Read it last, as confirmation, never as a trigger.

Chapter 15 — Part II

Alerts that actually work

The script hardcodes no alert conditions at all. It publishes level series and lets TradingView’s own dialog do the rest — which is more flexible than it first appears, and more limited than you want.

Nine series reach the Data Window; two of them are price levels you can build crossing alerts on:

SeriesUnitAlert-worthy conditions
Dashboard POCPriceCrossing — price entering the local centre of gravity
Chart POCPriceCrossing — the structural magnet being reached or lost

Three alerts worth building today

  1. Return to value. Chart POC — Crossing. Fires when price reaches the structural magnet from either side. Pair with the OVL regime read before acting.
  2. Regime flip. Balance Tilt % — Crossing up 15, and a second alert Crossing down −15. This is your “the period has taken a side” notification.
  3. Instrument health. Residual PPM — Greater than 1. Fires only if the profile arithmetic drifts. You want to know before you make a decision on a bad shape, not after.
What you cannot alert on yet

There is no series for the Value Area edges, no series for imbalance levels, and no alertcondition() anywhere in the script. If you want “price touched VAH” or “a stacked imbalance formed”, those must be added. Chapter 27 provides both, in about a dozen lines each.

Chapter 16 — Part III

Playbook: the POC magnet

Price that leaves the point of control without building new value tends to come back to it. This is the oldest idea in profile trading, and the instrument gives you the level and the regime filter in one panel.

Return to value

rotational regime only

Thesis. In a balanced market the POC is where business is being done. Price that wanders away from it without generating volume at the new price is an excursion, not a move, and the auction pulls it back.

Setup. OVL ≥ 0.75 (the label reads BALANCED). Price at or beyond a VA edge. The C‑POC has not moved materially over the last several bars — a POC that is itself migrating means value is relocating and this trade is void.

Entry

First bar that closes back inside the value area, in the direction of the POC

Invalidation

A close beyond the VA edge accompanied by a rising WIN Δ in the breakout direction

Target

C‑POC first; the far VA edge if OVL stays above threshold

Fails when

OVL was read on a period that is too short, or on a market full of flat bars inflating it

Chapter 17 — Part III

Playbook: the value-area edge

The same level, two opposite trades, separated entirely by whether volume builds on the far side.

An edge is not support or resistance. It is the boundary of where business got done. Price crossing it is not the event — price staying there and generating volume is the event. The instrument shows you exactly that, because the frame follows price: once price is outside the old value area, the window's Sum column starts filling at the new prices, and you can watch acceptance form in real time.

Rejection — fade the edge

balanced

Tell: price pokes beyond VAH or VAL; the new frame rows fill thinly; WIN Δ does not extend in the breakout direction; the W‑POC stays behind, inside the old band.

Entry

Close back inside the band

Invalidation

W‑POC migrates out to the new prices

Target

C‑POC, then the opposite edge

Fails when

You are actually in a trend and OVL was never checked

Acceptance — go with the edge

off balance

Tell: OVL below threshold with a named side; price beyond the edge and the window rows beyond it filling heavily; W‑POC has already followed price out; Balance Tilt agrees in sign.

Entry

Pullback that holds the broken edge, with the W‑POC now outside

Invalidation

Price re-enters the band and the W‑POC snaps back inside

Target

Measured move, or the next higher-timeframe C‑POC

Fails when

Profile Period is so short that “the band” is just the last few bars

The discriminator, stated once

Rejection and acceptance look identical for the first bar. The thing that separates them is whether the W‑POC follows price out of the band. Because the window is price-anchored, the W‑POC is the fastest acceptance detector in the instrument — it moves the moment real business starts happening at the new prices. This is the one place the sliding frame, normally a liability, is a genuine advantage.

Chapter 18 — Part III

Playbook: the imbalance shelf

An imbalanced level is a price where one side already overwhelmed the other. It is worth more as a stop location than as an entry signal.

Shelf as invalidation

works today

Use: you are already long from another signal. The metrics column shows ▲ IMB below your entry. Place the stop one tick below that level rather than at an arbitrary distance. You are now risking exactly the distance to “the buyers who pushed here were wrong.”

Entry

From your own signal — this playbook does not generate one

Invalidation

One tick beyond the imbalanced level

Target

Unchanged by this playbook

Fails when

Geometric engine on a wide-range bar — the “imbalance” is an artefact of bell placement, not aggression

Engine dependency is severe here

Imbalance is the metric most damaged by a modelled engine. In Geometric mode the diagonal comparison runs between two adjacent slices of a smooth analytic curve — and a smooth curve rising steeply always produces ratios above 3× somewhere on its flank. You are detecting the steepness of a bell, not the aggression of a buyer. Treat Geometric-mode imbalance as decorative. It becomes real information in Intrabar mode and authoritative in Footprint mode, where the venue’s own verdict is used.

Chapter 19 — Part III

Playbook: OVL as a regime filter

Not a trade. A gate that decides which of your trades are allowed to exist right now.

Almost every losing streak with profile tools comes from applying rotational logic in a trending market or trend logic in a range. OVL is the cheapest available answer to which one you are in, and it is derived from the volume itself rather than from price geometry.

OVLTiltLabelAllowedForbidden
≥ 0.75anyBALANCEDFade edges, target POC, mean reversion, range scalpsBreakout continuation
< 0.75≥ +5%OFF BALANCE TO BUYBuy pullbacks, hold through the POC, trend continuationShorting the VAH
< 0.75≤ −5%OFF BALANCE TO SELLSell rallies, hold through the POCBuying the VAL
< 0.75|tilt| < 5%OFF BALANCENothing. Two sides in separate territories, neither dominantEverything — this is a transition

That last row is the most valuable one and the easiest to skip. The market has left balance but has not chosen. This is where most breakout failures and most whipsaws live. The instrument is explicitly telling you it cannot name a direction; the correct response is to stop trading, not to guess.

Tuning the gate

Balance Tilt % sets how far apart the two sides must be before a side gets named. Raise it to 8–10 to hear only about clear dominance (fewer, better trend signals, more time in the “do nothing” state). Lower it to 2–3 to catch smaller leans. The 0.75 OVL threshold is hardcoded; if you want it configurable, that is a one-line input in Chapter 27.

Chapter 21 — Part III

Playbook: when W‑POC and C‑POC disagree

Two points of control, computed over different spans by different rules. Their relationship is a nested-timeframe read that no other single panel gives you.

The W‑POC is local and price-anchored; the C‑POC is structural and time-anchored. Both appear together only in Mobile Mode — which is a good reason to keep it on.

RelationshipReadingTrade posture
W‑POC ≈ C‑POCLocal business is happening exactly where the period’s business happened. Maximum agreement.Strong level. Fade approaches to it in balance; expect it to hold as support/resistance after a break.
W‑POC above C‑POCRecent participation has migrated up. Value is relocating higher before the period POC reflects it.Early trend signal. Favour longs; the C‑POC will drag upward if this persists.
W‑POC below C‑POCMirror — value relocating down.Favour shorts. Rallies into the C‑POC are selling opportunities while this holds.
W‑POC far from C‑POC, thin rowsPrice has travelled to somewhere nobody is trading. Excursion, not relocation.Expect reversion. Check the column headers — large offsets confirm nobody has been here.

The confirmation sequence for a genuine relocation is worth memorising, because it happens in a fixed order:

  1. Price leaves the value area.
  2. Window rows at the new prices begin filling — the Sum column grows outside the old band.
  3. W‑POC migrates to the new prices. This is the confirmation.
  4. Balance Tilt extends in the same direction.
  5. Only then does the C‑POC follow — typically many bars later.

If steps 2 and 3 do not happen, step 1 was an excursion and the POC magnet playbook applies instead.

Chapter 22 — Part III

The anti-playbook

Things this instrument will happily show you that you should not trade.

Do not trade

Geometric imbalance

You are measuring the steepness of an analytic curve. See Chapter 18.

Do not trade

Intra-bar banner flips

In Geometric mode the banner’s BUY/SELL verdict is a function of where the forming bar currently sits. It will invert repeatedly before close.

Do not trade

W‑POC as a fixed level

It moves when price moves, with no new trades required. Mark the C‑POC instead.

Do not trade

A VA edge pinned to the frame boundary

That is the edge of your view, not the edge of value. Widen the frame.

Do not trade

Any shape while RES reads DRIFT

The drawing is not a faithful picture of the volume behind it.

Do not trade

OVL on a market of flat bars

Atoms match perfectly and inflate the coefficient. A market that isn’t trading reads as “balanced”.

Do not trade

Columns with huge offsets

A header reading −847 is last month’s volume presented as current context.

Do not trade

Anything on a tick-count symbol

Spot FX and CFDs. The numbers are activity, not size.

Chapter 23 — Part IV

What this instrument does genuinely well

Credit where it is due, itemised — because knowing the real strengths tells you which questions to bring to it.

Strength 01

It never fabricates data

Missing engine data is na everywhere and is never silently replaced by zero or by another engine’s figure. Bars the engine cannot fill are excluded from the window rather than counted as empty. This discipline is unusual and it is the reason the numbers can be trusted as far as the engine can be.

Strength 02

The arithmetic is exact and self-audited

Row masses are closed-form integrals, never bucketed-and-rescaled. Off-frame volume is retained so every side re-adds to the bar’s real volume. And RES continuously proves it — a self-check that would be meaningless in a rescaled implementation.

Strength 03

Zone memory asks the better question

“The last five bars that traded here” is more useful than “the last five bars”. No mainstream footprint tool does this, and it is what makes the panel meaningful at a level price has just returned to.

Strength 04

One frame, full tick resolution

Nothing inside the frame is merged. You are looking at genuine per-tick structure around price, not a bucketed approximation of it — which is what makes the diagonal imbalance test meaningful at all.

Strength 05

Graceful degradation across plans

The same panel, the same metrics, the same layout on a free plan and on Ultimate. Only the provenance changes, and the banner tells you which provenance you have.

Strength 06

OVL is a real contribution

An overlap coefficient between the buy and sell distributions is a genuinely good regime classifier, computed properly (Simpson integration cut at every density crossing) rather than approximated. Most tools give you a delta and call it a day.

Strength 07

Alert design by restraint

Publishing level series instead of hardcoded conditions lets TradingView’s own dialog provide crossing, greater-than, and the rest. Fewer opinions in the script, more flexibility for you.

Strength 08

The mobile panel is a real design decision

Conclusions instead of data, recomputed with identical rules so the two views cannot disagree. Defaulting it on is the correct call and most authors get it backwards.

Chapter 24 — Part IV

Fourteen traps, and how to get out of each

Ordered by how much money each one is likely to cost you. Severity is honest: by design means it is a consequence of what the tool is; fixable means Chapter 27 has code; hard limit means neither you nor the author can remove it.

01 · The profile is always geometric fixable

Set the engine to Footprint and the profile still draws bells. The engine changes each bar's buy and sell totals, which feed the bell coefficients — but the bells' centres and widths come from high, low, close and Concentration, exactly as in Geometric mode. Real footprint rows are never used to shape the profile, so the C‑POC, the chart Value Area and the chart imbalances are all modelled quantities even on an Ultimate plan.

Until you patch it: in Footprint mode trust the table, which does read real rows, and treat the profile as a smoothed sketch. Cross-check the C‑POC against the Sum column — if the Sum column shows two clusters and the C‑POC sits between them, ignore the C‑POC entirely for that period.

02 · The frame slides, so window metrics are relative by design

Covered in Chapter 04. The W‑POC, dashboard VA and window imbalances all move when price moves, without any new trade occurring. Workaround: use them as local, short-horizon reads only, and mark structural levels from the C‑POC. Chapter 27 adds an optional anchored frame.

03 · No history, no backtest by design

The table and profile exist only on the last bar. You cannot scroll back to see what the panel said an hour ago, and no amount of Pine will change that cheaply — drawing them per-bar would exhaust object limits within a few hundred bars. Workaround: the raw series (Buy/Sell/Total Volume, Buy Share %, Balance Tilt) do exist on every bar and can be plotted, exported, and studied historically. Build your statistical work on those.

04 · One bell per side cannot be bimodal by design

A single bar's buy volume is one bell. A bar that traded heavily at its low, went quiet through the middle, and traded heavily at its high is drawn as a smooth hump over the middle. Across many bars the mixture can be multimodal — but only if the bars' ranges differ enough. On a period of similar bars covering a similar range, the mixture converges on a single hump no matter what really happened.

05 · Geometric’s close-location split is a guess hard limit

A bar closing on its low reports zero buy volume. A violent reversal that closes mid-range is indistinguishable from a doji. There is no repair inside the Geometric engine — that is what the engine is. Workaround: switch engines, or restrict yourself to the aggregate figures (Total Volume, and price-derived structure) which do not depend on the split.

06 · Off-frame volume is invisible fixable

Computed, retained, never shown. A column's Total can far exceed the sum of its visible cells with nothing on screen explaining the gap. Workaround today: widen the frame until Totals roughly match visible cells. See Chapter 27 for a two-line overflow indicator.

07 · Columns can be arbitrarily old fixable

No age limit exists; only the 5000-bar ceiling. Workaround today: read the headers, every time.

08 · Only the first imbalance each side is marked fixable

Stacked imbalances — the only ones worth trading — are structurally invisible. The per-cell arrows in the table are also gated to the same two rows, so even the full table will not reveal a stack. Workaround today: read the raw cell numbers and apply the 3× diagonal test by eye.

09 · The dashboard Value Area is clipped by the frame fixable

Expansion cannot cross the frame boundary, so a VA edge sitting on the top or bottom row is usually an artefact. Workaround today: widen Ticks Above/Below until both edges pull inward.

10 · Intrabar is wick-blind and history-limited hard limit

An LTF candle's whole volume goes to whichever side its close favoured, regardless of the excursion in between; and your plan caps how far back data exists. Workaround: drop the LTF (1s where available) to shrink the classification error, accept the reduced coverage, and read DATA n BARS before trusting the window.

11 · Two different CDF precisions in one script fixable

The table's normal CDF carries ~10−8 error; the profile's carries ~10−16. Harmless for trading, but it means table figures and profile band masses are not comparable to full precision, and RES only audits the profile side. One-line fix in Chapter 27.

12 · No cumulative delta, no VA or imbalance alerts fixable

Every delta is a snapshot; only two of the metrics reach the alert dialog as levels. Both gaps are small additions. See Chapter 27.

13 · Flat bars inflate OVL fixable

A flat bar contributes an atom to both sides at the same price, and matched atoms add directly to the overlap term. On illiquid symbols and dead sessions, OVL can read 0.9+ purely because nothing traded. Workaround today: check BARS n / m on the OVL label — and if a large share of the period is flat bars, distrust the coefficient.

14 · Cost grows fast with Period × Resolution fixable

Every update rebuilds the components, cuts the price axis at every bar boundary and every density crossing, and runs composite Simpson over each piece — with a bisection loop of up to 90 iterations per crossing. At Period 60 × Resolution 400 on a fast chart this becomes visibly slow and can hit Pine's execution limits. Workaround today: Resolution above ~150 buys no visible smoothness; leave it at 100.

Perspective

Eleven of these fourteen are fixable in the source, and most in under twenty lines. That is a good ratio — it means the architecture is sound and the gaps are features not yet written, not design errors. The three that are not fixable are honest consequences of what data is available on what plan.

Two changes that would make it a different product

Beyond the list above, two additions would move this from “an excellent modelled footprint” to something with no close equivalent:

  • Naked POC memory. Keep a rolling array of POCs from prior profile periods and draw the ones price has not yet returned to. Untested POCs are among the most reliable magnets in profile trading, and the script already computes a POC per update — it just throws the old ones away.
  • Absorption detection. A level with very high volume where price failed to progress is absorption; the same volume where price moved through is initiative. The instrument has both the per-level volume and the bar's price movement, so the classification costs almost nothing and identifies a genuinely different event from imbalance.

P1 · Real-row profile in Footprint mode

Accumulate the venue's own rows across the profile period into a lattice map, then read the POC and Value Area off that map instead of off the sampled bell mixture. Keep the bell path as the fallback for the other two engines.

// Accumulate real footprint rows over the profile period.
var map<int, float> profRowBuy  = map.new<int, float>()
var map<int, float> profRowSell = map.new<int, float>()

if barstate.islast and useFpEngine
    map.clear(profRowBuy)
    map.clear(profRowSell)
    for i = 0 to profPeriodInput - 1
        footprint fpI = engFp[i]
        if not na(fpI)
            for row in fpI.rows()
                int k = int(math.round(row.up_price() / syminfo.mintick))
                map.put(profRowBuy,  k, nz(map.get(profRowBuy,  k)) + row.buy_volume())
                map.put(profRowSell, k, nz(map.get(profRowSell, k)) + row.sell_volume())

    // Real POC: the lattice level with the most total volume.
    float bestVol = 0.0
    int   bestK   = na
    for k in map.keys(profRowBuy)
        float tot = nz(map.get(profRowBuy, k)) + nz(map.get(profRowSell, k))
        if tot > bestVol
            bestVol := tot
            bestK   := k
    if not na(bestK)
        pocChartLevel := bestK * syminfo.mintick
        pocChartBuy   := nz(map.get(profRowBuy,  bestK))
        pocChartSell  := nz(map.get(profRowSell, bestK))

The same map drives a real Value Area (expand from bestK over neighbouring keys) and, if you draw the profile from it as a step polyline rather than a smooth curve, a profile that finally shows double distributions.

ComponentOne bar’s truncated bell for one side, as a member of the profile mixture.
DeltaBuy volume minus sell volume. Available per bar, per window, never cumulative.
Diagonal imbalanceA level’s buy volume beating the sell volume one tick below (or the mirror), by the threshold.
LatticeThe whole-tick price grid. A price belongs to the row round(price / tick).
Off-frame volumeVolume a bar traded outside the frame. Retained in the arithmetic, never displayed.
OVLOverlap coefficient, 0–1: how much buying and selling happened at the same prices.
RESResidual, in parts per million: how far the profile’s own integrals drifted from the real volume.
TiltSigned share of the period’s volume favouring one side, in percentage points.
Truncation massThe share of a bell falling inside the bar’s range; each bell is renormalised by it.
W‑POCWindow point of control — from the table window. Local, and it slides.
Zone memoryThe search rule that fills columns only with bars that traded inside the frame.
